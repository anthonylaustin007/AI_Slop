# Failure Modes: Full Example Bank

Source: a professional grading rubric ("Rating Sections and Axes"), a failure-mode guide ("Professional Writing Failure Modes" / "Writing Quality Instructions"), and calibration exercises where reviewers labeled real model output sentence by sentence.

Scoring context: reviewers grade documents 0–5 on content and aesthetics. A document with any conspicuous slop is capped below passing (below 60/100); truly slop-free professional writing scores above 80. Slop cannot be offset by good formatting, and a major content failure (instruction-following miss, unsupported claim, fabricated citation) caps the whole document regardless of prose quality.

<a name="category-1"></a>
## 2. Category 1: Formulaic, slogan-like, or figurative language

**Flag when:** the underlying claim is understandable, but it is packaged as a stock formula, slogan, staged cadence, canned emotional phrase, or strained metaphor instead of clear analysis. Includes repeated colons, semicolons, or em dashes used to manufacture rhythm or emphasis rather than clarify meaning.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Inflated contrast:** "This isn't just a calendar - it's a gateway to a more intentional life." / "Data access is not a background detail. It's the heart of the user experience."
- **Odd words / strained metaphor:** "Proceed only when five readiness gates are green" (for diligence-deck criteria).
- **Overused parallelism/semicolons:** "The old request drew a boundary around hotspots; the new request removes that map layer." / "Early restrictions were zone-based; the late-December version removes the map as the control surface."
- **Unnecessary em dashes:** "Purpose: isolate what changed – and what deliberately stayed in place – under Osaka Prefecture's Red Stage emergency response."
- **Stock formula:** "The tool not only saves time, but also transforms how teams collaborate." / "faster, smarter, and more intuitive."
- **Slogan-like transition or fragment:** "From paper-bound practicals to a shared digital workspace." / "One team. One vision. Limitless possibilities." / "Win the close. Keep the evidence." / "Pipeline is flat. Spend isn't." / "EBITDA is not cash. Bridge it." / "This is not just another X. The better framing is Y." / "It's not X it's Y."
- **Overused colons:** "Universities: reinforce guidance. Students: reduce social activity. Everyone: keep the year end quieter."
- **Staged cadence:** "Different sectors, same behavioral logic: reduce optional contact where consequences are highest."
- **Canned empathy:** "I completely understand how frustrating and overwhelming this situation must feel."
- **Reader flattery as an opener:** "I have long admired your organization's unwavering commitment to innovation and excellence." / "Your team's groundbreaking work in this space speaks for itself." The reader already knows what they do; the sentence exists to warm them up, and readers of proposals, pitches, and letters recognize it as a template line. Open with the thing you are offering or asking for.
- **Synthetic balance without a real tradeoff:** "While remote work offers flexibility, it also presents unique challenges."
- **Mannered parallelism:** "The problem is clear: priorities are shifting; timelines are slipping; confidence is fading — and the moment for action is now."
- **Inflated significance:** turning mundane facts into claims about legacy, identity, broader trends, pivotal moments, or an "evolving landscape."
- **Promotional / travel-guide tone:** unrequested salesy praise; reflexive adjectives such as "vibrant," "rich," "renowned," "groundbreaking," "nestled."
- **Vague authorities / synthetic consensus:** "experts argue," "observers note," "scholars say," "several sources suggest."
- **Canned endings:** generic "challenges," "legacy," or "future outlook" conclusions that do not arise from the content.
- **Repeated rhetorical triads:** habitual sets of three adjectives, abstract nouns, clauses, or examples that make prose feel manufactured.
- **Overlong parallel enumerations:** piling up rhythmic catalogues of who/what/where clauses or abstract nouns to simulate exhaustiveness after the point is clear.
- **Repeated negative parallelism:** "not X, but Y," "not only X, but also Y," "not just X, but Y," "no X, no Y, just Z."
- **Dense clusters of AI-associated vocabulary:** delve, pivotal, robust, tapestry, underscore, showcase, foster, intricate, landscape, testament, vibrant. The full list, grouped by what each word is standing in for, is in [section 8](#vocabulary).
- **Mechanical bold-label bullet lists:** repeated bullets of the form "**Label:** explanation" when that structure is not useful or requested.

**Do not flag:** constructions that state concrete distinctions, give a clear warning, or quote an identified source. "The bug is in the parser, not the tokenizer." / "The red light means stop, and the green light means go." Parallel structure or punctuation that separates a real list, contrast, or logical relationship is fine.

<a name="category-2"></a>
## 3. Category 2: Vague, inflated, or unsupported substance

**Flag when:** the reader cannot tell what changed, why the benefit follows, what evidence supports the claim, or what reason drove the decision. The specific rationale cannot be recovered because evidence, causality, actors, or observable meaning are missing.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Empty abstraction:** "This unlocks value, fosters alignment, and drives meaningful impact." / "Labor costs push it; few have it; so it grows faster." / "Breadth plus intelligence, not the original module, is where growth now comes from."
- **Unclear meaning:** "Everyday computer work gets the same agentic loop." (What loop? Unrecoverable.)
- **Tacked-on benefit:** "The interface centralizes key information, ensuring a seamless user experience."
- **Inflated significance / unnamed authority:** "This represents a profound shift." / "Research consistently shows that this approach improves outcomes." (no source)
- **Informal shorthand that loses meaning:** "Data Center is doing the heavy lifting." / "The next guide resets the bar higher."
- **Process instead of reason:** "After several rounds of cross-functional review, we aligned on the next phase."
- **Oversimplification:** trimming "Where to draw the line on speed investments" to "Where to draw the line," or "When faster shipping drives growth rather than simply increasing costs" to "When faster shipping drives growth."

**Do not flag:** claims supported by a concrete result, source, constraint, or approval requirement. "The change removes one approval step." / "The 12 June accessibility audit found 14 missing labels." / "Legal and Security must approve the exception before release."

**Revision move:** name the observable change, source, deciding constraint, or actual tradeoff.

<a name="category-3"></a>
## 4. Category 3: Wordy, jargon-filled, or indirect language

**Flag when:** the sentence can be shorter and clearer without losing necessary meaning or a real qualification. The rationale is clear but the wording is unnecessarily long, indirect, compressed, bureaucratic, jargon-heavy, or hedged.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Corporate/bureaucratic phrasing:** "Stakeholders should be informed of the operational implications associated with this transition."
- **Overcomplicated sentence structure:** "The clean end state is therefore not 'CCA replaces every product.' It is: shared primitives provide durable identity and lifecycle; CCA provides portable agent execution; each surface becomes an orientation onto that shared graph." → "CCA provides a portable agent execution capability that every surface can reuse, alongside shared identity and lifecycle primitives."
- **Compressed abstraction:** "The practical event ceiling remains anchored to both a headcount cap and a percentage cap."
- **Indirect comparison:** "The update reads as a broader continuation of requests rather than a list of named restricted zones."
- **Overly hedging:** "It may potentially be worth considering whether the team could possibly delay the launch."
- **Unnecessary verbosity:** "At this point in time, it would be advisable for the team to begin the process of reviewing the draft."
- **Unexplained jargon:** "The workflow operationalizes a cross-functional enablement layer for downstream value realization."

**Do not flag:** accurate technical terms, legal conditions, or explained uncertainty. "The API returns 429 when the client exceeds the rate limit." / "The estimate is preliminary because two regions have not reported." / "The vendor may terminate only after giving 30 days' written notice."

**Revision move:** use concrete subjects and verbs. Keep the shortest accurate wording and only the uncertainty markers that correspond to real unknowns.

<a name="category-4"></a>
## 5. Category 4: Unnecessary framing, repetition, or structure

**Flag when:** setup, repetition, or formatting delays the point or makes the document harder to scan.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Generic scene-setting:** "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, effective communication is more important than ever."
- **Restating the request:** "When it comes to improving employee onboarding, there are several strategies to consider."
- **Echoing the brief:** the request's own phrases returned as content. A brief asks for "a customer obsessed, data driven plan" and the reply opens "This customer obsessed, data driven plan..." The reader wrote those words and learns nothing from seeing them again. Replace the echo with what the plan does: "The plan moves the two lowest rated support queues to same day response."
- **Meta-announcement:** "Below is a polished and comprehensive rewrite tailored to your needs."
- **Redundant conclusion:** "In conclusion, adopting these strategies can help organizations achieve their goals."
- **Excessive structure:** a two-sentence answer split across six headings and twelve bullets.

**Do not flag:** framing that narrows scope, corrects the request, explains an omission, or helps readers navigate reference material. "This memo covers the two launch decisions due Friday." / "Each API endpoint uses Request, Response, and Errors headings for lookup."

**Revision move:** start with the answer or decision. Delete generic setup and repeated recaps. Use the lightest structure that helps the reader act or find information.

<a name="calibration-cases"></a>
## 6. Calibration cases: borderline sentences, labeled

From exercises where reviewers labeled real model output. Useful for judging edge cases.

**Labeled SLOP:**

- "From paper-bound practicals to a shared digital workspace." — transformation arc that sounds impressive but never states what changed.
- "The workflow operationalizes a cross-functional enablement layer for downstream value realization." — hides actor and action behind abstract business terms.
- "This represents a profound shift." — asserts significance without a concrete change or evidence.
- "A finite stock is being consumed by an annual flow." (subtitle on a carbon-budget chart) — unclear; there is a plainer way to say it.
- "Different sectors, same behavioral logic: reduce optional contact where consequences are highest." — staged cadence summary line.
- "Use a 90-day corridor cohort to prove that better vetting, monitoring, and service management lift execution and contribution per load; expand regional lanes only after those gates are met." — jargon-dense, gate metaphor.
- "Reliability and risk control are shipper value, not back-office hygiene." — aphoristic contrast in place of analysis.
- "Quality improvements compound across existing volume and future lanes." — abstract claim, no observable support.
- "Uber Freight's latest Q2 update says first-tender acceptance fell to 82% in April, spot rates were more than 25% higher year over year, and cargo theft was becoming more sophisticated; shocks were showing up lane by lane." — overlong enumeration with a vague coda ("shocks were showing up lane by lane").
- "A measured deployment can create meaningful cohort-level value, but the first year is deliberately dilutive and the downside remains negative without verified adoption and incrementality." — abstraction stacked on abstraction.
- "Public filings anchor the baseline. Controlled experiments must earn the forecast." — staged two-beat cadence; meaning must be reverse-engineered.
- "Win with a CFO-sponsored, controller-enabled, savings-backed deployment—not with generic AI messaging." — slogan with stacked compound modifiers.
- "Spend information is fragmented: ERP, AP, cards, procurement, contracts and spreadsheets produce late insight and weak action ownership." — list-heavy compression; "late insight and weak action ownership" is unrecoverable.
- "The market is crowded: feature novelty is insufficient; the launch must prove governed outcomes in the client's own data." — colon-semicolon cadence, abstract jargon.

**Labeled NOT slop (keep sentences like these):**

- "The bug is in the parser, not the tokenizer." — concrete distinction.
- "The 12 June accessibility audit found 14 missing labels." — source and result.
- "The API returns 429 when the client exceeds the rate limit." — technical term, concrete condition.
- "At the 2018 emissions pace, 1.5°C has roughly 10 years left." — number-backed headline.
- "Prioritize carrier quality over new lane expansion or shipper-onboarding automation." — direct recommendation with named alternatives.
- "Q1 2026 Freight gross bookings and revenue were $1.334 billion and $1.337 billion, respectively, but segment operating loss widened to $30 million." — figures.
- "In 2025, revenue fell 1%, while adjusted EBITDA improved 55% as carrier payments and other expenses declined." — figures with cause.
- "Uber Freight reports working with more than 125,000 vetted truckload carriers and one-third of Fortune 500 companies." — attributed figures.
- "In Q1, fraud incidents fell 20% after tighter carrier onboarding, monitoring, and detection." — result plus mechanism.
- "Regional lanes are the next bet only where validated carrier depth already exists." — concrete condition.
- "Optimize unit economics before adding footprint." — terse but recoverable directive.
- "Approve a stage-gated pilot; do not pre-authorize a broad rollout." — decision language with a real distinction.
- "The investment has a modeled three-year net present value of $18 million, using a 12% planning hurdle." — figures and method.
- "The modeled economic payback period is 21 months, including training time." — figure with scope.
- "CFOs need to reduce expenses, improve forecast accuracy, and strengthen financial controls." — plain list of real goals.
- "Identify which executives approve the purchase and which teams will use the product." — actionable and concrete.
- "Restrict nonessential visits; strengthen screening." — plain directive (semicolon separating two real actions).

The dividing line: sentences that survive carry a number, a named source, a real condition, or a decision. Sentences that get flagged carry cadence, abstraction, or compression the reader must decode.

<a name="formatting"></a>
## 7. Formatting and overstyling reference

From the aesthetics rubric (priority order: visual defects > overstyling > skimmability):

**Visual defects (worst — an unusable defect caps the score at 0):** clipped/overlapping/corrupted content, content crossing container boundaries, unreadable text, broken margins/alignment/spacing/page flow, low contrast, broken repeated elements, visible editing residue, manual text-shaped numbering ("1 |") instead of native lists, misaligned tables/callouts.

**Overstyling (remove anything that can be removed without reducing clarity):**

- decorative lines beneath headers, between sections, or at the end of the document
- colored callout blocks, bright accents, colored headings and table fills
- three-part title treatments (eyebrow + main title + subtitle)
- tables where a simple list would be clearer
- zebra striping, multiple fill colors, heavy borders, decorative framing
- charts, diagrams, or images added for decoration rather than understanding
- repeated middle dots, pipes, or decorative separators

Most workplace documents: minimal structure, mostly black text on white, easy to read and edit. Tables, diagrams, and headers default to simple black-and-white with plain borders; use color or shading only when it improves comparison, grouping, or readability. Extra styling is justified only when the user requested it or it is standard for the document type and audience.

**Skimmability:** clear headings, readable paragraphs, useful white space, and native elements (bullets, numbered lists, checkboxes) where they help — judged against the document type. A research report should be skimmable; a legal brief is legitimately dense.

**Overall document judgment:** score holistically, not as an average. A major failure (instruction-following miss, unsupported claim, fabricated or missing required citation, omission that prevents professional use) caps the document regardless of strengths elsewhere. Ask: is it ready for real use? Does it succeed for the apparent audience and purpose? How much revision before it could be confidently shared?

<a name="vocabulary"></a>
## 8. Vocabulary: the words, grouped by what they hide

None of these words is banned, and a list of banned words would not work anyway: a model told to avoid "leverage" writes "harness." What the groups below share is a job. Each word lets a sentence sound complete while leaving out the fact a reader would need to check it. So the test is not "is the word on the list" but "what plain word or fact is it standing in for," and the paragraph-level signal is density: three or more of these in a paragraph that carries no number, source, or decision means the paragraph is made of them.

**Verbs that inflate an ordinary action.** leverage, utilize, harness, unlock, empower, elevate, streamline, spearhead, bolster, foster, facilitate, drive, navigate, delve, underscore, showcase, embark, revolutionize, transform, optimize, ensure, enable.

| Slop | Better |
|---|---|
| We leveraged cross-functional partnerships to drive alignment on the roadmap. | We asked Sales for the churn list and cut two roadmap items it did not support. |
| The team spearheaded an initiative to streamline onboarding. | The team cut onboarding from nine forms to four. |
| This update empowers users to unlock deeper insights. | This update adds a filter by region. |

The verb is doing the work a result should do. Ask what the person did with their hands or their calendar, and write that.

**Adjectives that assert quality with the evidence removed.** robust, seamless, comprehensive, holistic, transformative, innovative, cutting edge, dynamic, pivotal, crucial, critical, key, vital, essential, meaningful, impactful, actionable, scalable, powerful, strategic, intricate, nuanced, multifaceted, vibrant, rich, renowned, groundbreaking, world class, best in class, state of the art, ever evolving.

| Slop | Better |
|---|---|
| We built a robust, scalable pipeline. | The pipeline ran through the Black Friday peak of 40,000 orders an hour without a retry. |
| A comprehensive review of the vendor landscape | We compared the four vendors that answered the RFP. |
| A pivotal moment for the company | The first quarter the company was profitable. |

Each adjective is a conclusion. Write down what it was drawn from and the adjective usually becomes unnecessary; if it was drawn from nothing, it was the whole claim, and it goes.

**Nouns that gesture at scale instead of naming the thing.** landscape, tapestry, testament, journey, realm, ecosystem, synergy, paradigm, cornerstone, beacon, game changer, north star, framework, solution, insights, value, impact, outcomes, alignment, stakeholders, the space.

| Slop | Better |
|---|---|
| The new pricing is a cornerstone of our value creation journey. | The new pricing raises the starter plan from $19 to $29. |
| We shared the results with stakeholders across the ecosystem. | We sent the results to the three account managers and the CFO. |
| The migration delivered meaningful outcomes for the business. | The migration cut the nightly batch from six hours to forty minutes. |

Abstract nouns are placeholders for a name, a number, or a person. Find which one and substitute it.

**Stock phrases that occupy the slot where the point should be.** at its core, at the end of the day, it's worth noting, it's important to note, in today's world, in the ever evolving, in the realm of, dive into, deep dive, a wide range of, plays a crucial role, serves as, stands as, boasts, is a testament to, moving forward, going forward, navigate the complexities, the intersection of, when it comes to, whether it's X or Y, look no further, rest assured, I hope this finds you well, let's dive in, in a world where.

| Slop | Better |
|---|---|
| It's worth noting that at its core, the product serves as a bridge between teams. | The product lets Support see the same ticket Engineering sees. |
| Moving forward, we will navigate the complexities of the new regulation. | The new regulation requires consent before we store location data, so the signup form gets a checkbox in March. |
| Whether you're a small startup or a global enterprise, the platform meets you where you are. | The platform has a free tier and an enterprise tier. |

Delete the phrase and read the sentence again: if it still stands, the phrase was filler, and if it collapses, it was hiding the fact that there was no sentence.

**Transitions doing work the sentences should do on their own.** Additionally, Moreover, Furthermore, Firstly, Secondly, Lastly, Ultimately, Overall, In summary, In conclusion, In essence, Notably, Importantly, Crucially, Interestingly, That said, That being said, Simply put.

| Slop | Better |
|---|---|
| We reduced latency. Additionally, we cut costs. Moreover, uptime improved. | Latency, cost, and uptime all improved after the move to the new region: p95 fell from 800ms to 300ms, the bill dropped 22%, and there were no incidents in Q2. |
| Firstly, the vendor is expensive. Secondly, the vendor is slow. Lastly, the vendor is hard to reach. | The vendor costs 40% more than the alternative, took eleven days on the last ticket, and has no phone support. |

A paragraph that needs Moreover three times is a list wearing prose. Either make it a list, or find the relationship between the sentences (they share a cause, they trade off, one follows from another) and state that relationship instead of a connective.

**Do not flag:** any of these words used for its literal meaning or as a term of art, such as "landscape" in a piece about land, "robust standard errors" in statistics, "critical path" in a schedule, "framework" when it is React or Django, "optimize" when there is a stated objective and a measurement, "journey" in "customer journey map" when that is the artifact's name, and "ensure" when it describes a mechanism ("the check ensures the file exists before the job starts"). The word is not the problem; the missing fact behind it is, and a sentence that carries its fact can use whatever word it likes.

<a name="cadence"></a>
## 9. Multi-sentence cadence — the cases every other example here misses

Every example above is one sentence. These are not, and that is the point: read
any single sentence in the "before" columns and it passes. The slop is the shape
across them. All three shipped past a writer who had this skill open, which is
why the cadence check in SKILL.md is mechanical rather than a matter of noticing.

### Staged reveal

Sub-pattern: *staged cadence* (failure mode 1), plus a colon used for rhythm.

**Before:** "Five. That is the threshold sleep researchers use to call a bedtime
routine consistent, and the effect is graded: each additional night is associated
with better sleep than the night before it."

**After:** "Sleep researchers treat five nights a week as the point at which a
bedtime routine counts as consistent. Below that the association with better
sleep weakens, and it keeps improving above it."

Two tells. The one-word opener withholds a number for a beat, and the colon after
"graded" manufactures a second one. Neither adds information — the after version
carries strictly more, because "below that / above it" says which direction the
effect runs and the original only gestured at it.

Caught by: check 3 (one-word sentence).

### Parallel-sentence block

Sub-patterns: *staged cadence* and *rhetorical triad* (failure mode 1), and
*restating what the reader just read* (failure mode 4).

**Before:** "Two sentences, sitting next to each other. The first is evidence
about a practice. The second describes what the product is. The inference between
them belongs to the parent, and we do not make it for them."

**After:** "The first sentence is evidence about reading aloud. The second says
what HearLoom is. We stop there rather than joining them, because the study
behind the first sentence did not test the second."

This one sat directly beneath a two-sentence quote, so three of its four
sentences described something the reader had finished reading a moment earlier.
The after version keeps the two that orient and replaces the cadence with the
actual reason — the study did not test the product — which the original never
stated at all.

Caught by: check 1 (four short sentences in a row), check 2 ("The first… The
second… The inference…"), check 4 (its subjects are *sentence, first, second,
inference*).

### Heading pattern broken for effect

Sub-pattern: *staged cadence* applied to document structure. Borderline, and
worth saying why it lands on the slop side: the first two headings are genuine
parallelism of the kind SKILL.md protects, so only the one that abandons the
pattern for a beat is flagged.

**Before:** "## Writing: the constraint problem" / "## Illustrating: the
consistency problem" / "## Narrating: the point"

**After:** the first two unchanged; the third becomes "## Narrating: cloning a
real voice".

The first two name a real engineering problem each and are worth keeping — this
is the parallelism SKILL.md says to protect. The third abandons the pattern to
land a beat, and the sentence beneath it did the same ("the voice is the reason
the product exists"). A reader looking for what the narration step *does* gets
a flourish instead.

Caught by: check 4, applied to headings — "the point" describes the document's
argument rather than the subject matter.


### Do not flag: a paragraph that is legitimately about the text

"The first sentence is evidence about reading aloud. The second says what
HearLoom is. We stop there rather than joining them, because the study behind
the first sentence did not test the second."

`scripts/cadence.py` flags this twice — repeated opener "The", and check 4,
because its subjects are *first, second, sentence*. Both are correct readings
and the paragraph still survives, because it sits directly beneath a
two-sentence quote and its job is to say what those two sentences do and do not
license. The meta-vocabulary is the subject matter rather than a tic, and the
final clause carries a fact the reader cannot get anywhere else: no study behind
that policy tested this product.

This is the shape of a true positive that should not be edited. Kept here so the
checks are not read as a linter whose output must go to zero.

<a name="full-documents"></a>
## 10. Full-document rewrites: two documents, every cut labeled

Every example above is a sentence or a short run of them. A whole document fails in ways a sentence cannot show: whole paragraphs that carry no fact and get cut entirely, the one real number sitting in the middle where the writer got around to it, and a vague sentence covering for a fact the writer did not have. The two rewrites below show all three. Neither is a special genre; one is an internal status memo and one is an email to a customer.

Both rewrites add nothing. Every number, name, date, and decision in the "after" is in the "before." Where the original hid a missing fact, the rewrite shows a bracket instead of inventing one.

### A. Internal status memo

**Before** (245 words):

> **Subject:** Q3 CRM vendor consolidation update
>
> In today's rapidly evolving business landscape, vendor management has become more critical than ever. Over the past quarter our team has been on a journey to streamline our CRM footprint, and I'm excited to share where we've landed.
>
> At its core, this initiative was never just about cost savings. It was about empowering our teams with a seamless, unified platform that fosters alignment and drives meaningful impact across the organization. After extensive cross-functional collaboration, we have made significant progress toward that vision.
>
> Key highlights: We successfully leveraged our existing relationship with Vendor A to negotiate a robust enterprise agreement, reducing annual spend by 18%. Additionally, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of Vendor B and Vendor C, ultimately determining that Vendor C's cutting edge capabilities, including native integration with our billing system, align more closely with our strategic objectives. Moreover, the migration of the sales team's 41,000 pipeline records was completed two weeks ahead of schedule, a testament to the dedication of everyone involved.
>
> That said, some challenges remain. The marketing team's integration is proving more intricate than anticipated, and we are actively navigating those complexities. Rest assured, we are fully committed to ensuring a smooth transition.
>
> Moving forward, our focus will be on finalizing the marketing integration and unlocking the full value of the consolidated platform. This isn't just a vendor change. It's a foundation for how we'll work together.
>
> Please don't hesitate to reach out with any questions.

**After** (83 words):

> **Subject:** Q3 CRM vendor consolidation: sales done, marketing behind
>
> The new Vendor A enterprise agreement is signed and cuts our annual CRM spend by 18%. Sales has finished migrating. Marketing's integration is behind plan.
>
> The sales team's 41,000 pipeline records moved to the new platform two weeks ahead of schedule. We evaluated Vendor B and Vendor C and chose C because it integrates natively with our billing system.
>
> Marketing's integration is behind because [reason]. The revised completion date is [date].

**What was cut, by failure mode:**

- Paragraph 1, all of it. Generic scene setting ("In today's rapidly evolving business landscape," mode 4), two vocabulary words ("journey," "streamline"), and canned emotion ("I'm excited to share," mode 1). It contained no fact.
- Paragraph 2, all of it. "Never just about X. It was about Y" is negative parallelism (mode 1). "Empowering," "seamless," "unified," "fosters alignment," "drives meaningful impact" are five vocabulary items in one sentence with nothing to check (mode 2). "After extensive cross-functional collaboration, we have made significant progress" is process in place of a reason, plus unsupported significance (mode 2). It also contained no fact.
- Paragraph 3 held all three facts, each wrapped. The 18% came inside "successfully leveraged our existing relationship" and "robust." The billing integration, which was the real reason for choosing Vendor C, sat in a subordinate clause under "comprehensive," "cutting edge," and "align more closely with our strategic objectives." The 41,000 records had "a testament to the dedication of everyone involved" attached. "Additionally" and "Moreover" were joining three unrelated facts (mode 3); the rewrite gives each its own sentence.
- Paragraph 4. "That said, some challenges remain" is framing (mode 4). "Intricate" and "actively navigating those complexities" hide what is wrong (mode 2). "Rest assured, we are fully committed to ensuring a smooth transition" is canned reassurance (mode 1). This paragraph is where the memo was hiding a gap; see below.
- Paragraph 5. "Moving forward" and "unlocking the full value" (vocabulary, mode 2). "This isn't just a vendor change. It's a foundation for how we'll work together" is an inflated contrast used as a closing beat (mode 1), and the cadence check flags the short pair on its own. "Please don't hesitate to reach out" is a canned ending (mode 4).
- The lead moved. The 18% was in the sixth sentence of the original and is the first sentence of the rewrite. The subject line now says what happened instead of naming the topic.

**What survived:** every number (18%, 41,000, two weeks), every decision (Vendor A signed, C over B, and why), and the status of both migrations.

**What a rewrite cannot fix:** the original never said why marketing is behind or when it will finish, and "navigating those complexities" was standing in for both. The rewrite leaves two brackets rather than inventing a reason or a date; SKILL.md's rule is never to fabricate support. Either the writer fills the brackets or the memo goes out with the gap visible, and both are better than the sentence that hid it. Two of the memo's five paragraphs existed to cover for two facts the writer did not have.

### B. Email to a customer

**Before** (164 words):

> **Subject:** Exciting news: introducing our brand new analytics dashboard!
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I hope this email finds you well. We're thrilled to announce the launch of our completely redesigned analytics dashboard, a game changer for how you'll interact with your data.
>
> We've listened to your feedback and built a powerful, intuitive experience from the ground up. With faster load times, a streamlined layout, and a robust set of new filters, the new dashboard empowers you to unlock insights faster than ever before. Whether you're tracking daily performance or preparing for your quarterly review, it's designed to meet you where you are.
>
> The new dashboard will roll out to all accounts starting Monday, September 14. No action is needed on your part. Your existing saved reports will be migrated automatically, though custom date ranges will need to be re-entered.
>
> We can't wait for you to experience it. As always, don't hesitate to reach out to our support team with any questions.
>
> Warm regards,
> The Product Team

**After** (74 words):

> **Subject:** Your analytics dashboard changes on September 14
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> On Monday, September 14 your analytics dashboard will be replaced with a new version. Pages load faster, the layout is simpler, and there are new filters.
>
> Your saved reports will carry over. Custom date ranges will not, so you will need to re-enter them after the 14th. Nothing else is required from you.
>
> Questions go to our support team at [address].
>
> The Product Team

**What was cut, by failure mode:**

- "I hope this email finds you well" (stock opener, mode 4) and "We're thrilled to announce" (canned emotion, mode 1).
- "Game changer" (vocabulary) and "completely redesigned ... from the ground up" (unsupported significance, mode 2).
- "We've listened to your feedback" (mode 2). It is a claim about the reader's own input with nothing attached. It survives only if the email says which change came from which request.
- "Powerful," "intuitive," "robust" (adjectives with the evidence removed) and "empowers you to unlock insights faster than ever before" (empty abstraction, three vocabulary items in nine words, mode 2).
- "Whether you're tracking daily performance or preparing for your quarterly review, it's designed to meet you where you are" (stock frame, mode 1). It names two situations and says nothing about either.
- "We can't wait for you to experience it" (canned emotion, mode 1) and "As always, don't hesitate to reach out" (canned ending, mode 4).
- The subject line. The original's says nothing a reader could act on; the rewrite's carries the date.
- The fact the reader most needs, that custom date ranges will be lost, was a "though" clause at the end of the third paragraph. It now has its own sentence, with the action ("re-enter them after the 14th") stated.

**What survived:** the date, the three changes, the fact that no action is needed, and both migration facts. "Faster" and "simpler" stay because the source claims them; if the writer has the load times, they belong in that sentence in place of "faster."

### Using these on your own draft

List every number, name, date, and decision in the draft. Then read it again and mark each sentence that contains none of them. Those sentences are the candidates, and in both documents above they were most of the text. The rewrites removed two thirds of the memo, over half of the email, and none of the facts. When a rewrite shrinks a document by that much, the original was mostly slop. When it barely shrinks, either the original was clean or the rewrite was timid, and it is worth checking which.
