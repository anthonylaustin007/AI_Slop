# Failure Modes: Full Example Bank

Source: a professional grading rubric ("Rating Sections and Axes"), a failure-mode guide ("Professional Writing Failure Modes" / "Writing Quality Instructions"), and calibration exercises where reviewers labeled real model output sentence by sentence.

Scoring context: reviewers grade documents 0–5 on content and aesthetics. A document with any conspicuous slop is capped below passing (below 60/100); truly slop-free professional writing scores above 80. Slop cannot be offset by good formatting, and a major content failure (instruction-following miss, unsupported claim, fabricated citation) caps the whole document regardless of prose quality.

<a name="category-1"></a>
## 2. Category 1: Formulaic, slogan-like, or figurative language

**Flag when:** the underlying claim is understandable, but it is packaged as a stock formula, slogan, staged cadence, canned emotional phrase, or strained metaphor instead of clear analysis. Includes repeated colons, semicolons, or em dashes used to manufacture rhythm or emphasis rather than clarify meaning.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Inflated contrast:** "This isn't just a calendar - it's a gateway to a more intentional life." / "Data access is not a background detail. It's the heart of the user experience."
- **Odd words / strained metaphor:** "Proceed only when five readiness gates are green" (for diligence-deck criteria).
- **Overused parallelism/semicolons:** "The old request drew a boundary around hotspots; the new request removes that map layer." / "Early restrictions were zone-based; the late-December version removes the map as the control surface."
- **Unnecessary em dashes:** "Purpose: isolate what changed – and what deliberately stayed in place – under Osaka Prefecture's Red Stage emergency response."
- **Stock formula:** "The tool not only saves time, but also transforms how teams collaborate." / "faster, smarter, and more intuitive."
- **Slogan-like transition or fragment:** "From paper-bound practicals to a shared digital workspace." / "One team. One vision. Limitless possibilities." / "Win the close. Keep the evidence." / "Pipeline is flat. Spend isn't." / "EBITDA is not cash. Bridge it." / "This is not just another X. The better framing is Y." / "It's not X it's Y."
- **Overused colons:** "Universities: reinforce guidance. Students: reduce social activity. Everyone: keep the year end quieter."
- **Staged cadence:** "Different sectors, same behavioral logic: reduce optional contact where consequences are highest."
- **Canned empathy:** "I completely understand how frustrating and overwhelming this situation must feel."
- **Synthetic balance without a real tradeoff:** "While remote work offers flexibility, it also presents unique challenges."
- **Mannered parallelism:** "The problem is clear: priorities are shifting; timelines are slipping; confidence is fading — and the moment for action is now."
- **Inflated significance:** turning mundane facts into claims about legacy, identity, broader trends, pivotal moments, or an "evolving landscape."
- **Promotional / travel-guide tone:** unrequested salesy praise; reflexive adjectives such as "vibrant," "rich," "renowned," "groundbreaking," "nestled."
- **Vague authorities / synthetic consensus:** "experts argue," "observers note," "scholars say," "several sources suggest."
- **Canned endings:** generic "challenges," "legacy," or "future outlook" conclusions that do not arise from the content.
- **Repeated rhetorical triads:** habitual sets of three adjectives, abstract nouns, clauses, or examples that make prose feel manufactured.
- **Overlong parallel enumerations:** piling up rhythmic catalogues of who/what/where clauses or abstract nouns to simulate exhaustiveness after the point is clear.
- **Repeated negative parallelism:** "not X, but Y," "not only X, but also Y," "not just X, but Y," "no X, no Y, just Z."
- **Dense clusters of AI-associated vocabulary:** delve, pivotal, robust, tapestry, underscore, showcase, foster, intricate, landscape, testament, vibrant.
- **Mechanical bold-label bullet lists:** repeated bullets of the form "**Label:** explanation" when that structure is not useful or requested.

**Do not flag:** constructions that state concrete distinctions, give a clear warning, or quote an identified source. "The bug is in the parser, not the tokenizer." / "The red light means stop, and the green light means go." Parallel structure or punctuation that separates a real list, contrast, or logical relationship is fine.

<a name="category-2"></a>
## 3. Category 2: Vague, inflated, or unsupported substance

**Flag when:** the reader cannot tell what changed, why the benefit follows, what evidence supports the claim, or what reason drove the decision. The specific rationale cannot be recovered because evidence, causality, actors, or observable meaning are missing.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Empty abstraction:** "This unlocks value, fosters alignment, and drives meaningful impact." / "Labor costs push it; few have it; so it grows faster." / "Breadth plus intelligence, not the original module, is where growth now comes from."
- **Unclear meaning:** "Everyday computer work gets the same agentic loop." (What loop? Unrecoverable.)
- **Tacked-on benefit:** "The interface centralizes key information, ensuring a seamless user experience."
- **Inflated significance / unnamed authority:** "This represents a profound shift." / "Research consistently shows that this approach improves outcomes." (no source)
- **Informal shorthand that loses meaning:** "Data Center is doing the heavy lifting." / "The next guide resets the bar higher."
- **Process instead of reason:** "After several rounds of cross-functional review, we aligned on the next phase."
- **Oversimplification:** trimming "Where to draw the line on speed investments" to "Where to draw the line," or "When faster shipping drives growth rather than simply increasing costs" to "When faster shipping drives growth."

**Do not flag:** claims supported by a concrete result, source, constraint, or approval requirement. "The change removes one approval step." / "The 12 June accessibility audit found 14 missing labels." / "Legal and Security must approve the exception before release."

**Revision move:** name the observable change, source, deciding constraint, or actual tradeoff.

<a name="category-3"></a>
## 4. Category 3: Wordy, jargon-filled, or indirect language

**Flag when:** the sentence can be shorter and clearer without losing necessary meaning or a real qualification. The rationale is clear but the wording is unnecessarily long, indirect, compressed, bureaucratic, jargon-heavy, or hedged.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Corporate/bureaucratic phrasing:** "Stakeholders should be informed of the operational implications associated with this transition."
- **Overcomplicated sentence structure:** "The clean end state is therefore not 'CCA replaces every product.' It is: shared primitives provide durable identity and lifecycle; CCA provides portable agent execution; each surface becomes an orientation onto that shared graph." → "CCA provides a portable agent execution capability that every surface can reuse, alongside shared identity and lifecycle primitives."
- **Compressed abstraction:** "The practical event ceiling remains anchored to both a headcount cap and a percentage cap."
- **Indirect comparison:** "The update reads as a broader continuation of requests rather than a list of named restricted zones."
- **Overly hedging:** "It may potentially be worth considering whether the team could possibly delay the launch."
- **Unnecessary verbosity:** "At this point in time, it would be advisable for the team to begin the process of reviewing the draft."
- **Unexplained jargon:** "The workflow operationalizes a cross-functional enablement layer for downstream value realization."

**Do not flag:** accurate technical terms, legal conditions, or explained uncertainty. "The API returns 429 when the client exceeds the rate limit." / "The estimate is preliminary because two regions have not reported." / "The vendor may terminate only after giving 30 days' written notice."

**Revision move:** use concrete subjects and verbs. Keep the shortest accurate wording and only the uncertainty markers that correspond to real unknowns.

<a name="category-4"></a>
## 5. Category 4: Unnecessary framing, repetition, or structure

**Flag when:** setup, repetition, or formatting delays the point or makes the document harder to scan.

Bad examples by sub-pattern:

- **Generic scene-setting:** "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, effective communication is more important than ever."
- **Restating the request:** "When it comes to improving employee onboarding, there are several strategies to consider."
- **Meta-announcement:** "Below is a polished and comprehensive rewrite tailored to your needs."
- **Redundant conclusion:** "In conclusion, adopting these strategies can help organizations achieve their goals."
- **Excessive structure:** a two-sentence answer split across six headings and twelve bullets.

**Do not flag:** framing that narrows scope, corrects the request, explains an omission, or helps readers navigate reference material. "This memo covers the two launch decisions due Friday." / "Each API endpoint uses Request, Response, and Errors headings for lookup."

**Revision move:** start with the answer or decision. Delete generic setup and repeated recaps. Use the lightest structure that helps the reader act or find information.

## 6. Calibration cases: borderline sentences, labeled

From exercises where reviewers labeled real model output. Useful for judging edge cases.

**Labeled SLOP:**

- "From paper-bound practicals to a shared digital workspace." — transformation arc that sounds impressive but never states what changed.
- "The workflow operationalizes a cross-functional enablement layer for downstream value realization." — hides actor and action behind abstract business terms.
- "This represents a profound shift." — asserts significance without a concrete change or evidence.
- "A finite stock is being consumed by an annual flow." (subtitle on a carbon-budget chart) — unclear; there is a plainer way to say it.
- "Different sectors, same behavioral logic: reduce optional contact where consequences are highest." — staged cadence summary line.
- "Use a 90-day corridor cohort to prove that better vetting, monitoring, and service management lift execution and contribution per load; expand regional lanes only after those gates are met." — jargon-dense, gate metaphor.
- "Reliability and risk control are shipper value, not back-office hygiene." — aphoristic contrast in place of analysis.
- "Quality improvements compound across existing volume and future lanes." — abstract claim, no observable support.
- "Uber Freight's latest Q2 update says first-tender acceptance fell to 82% in April, spot rates were more than 25% higher year over year, and cargo theft was becoming more sophisticated; shocks were showing up lane by lane." — overlong enumeration with a vague coda ("shocks were showing up lane by lane").
- "A measured deployment can create meaningful cohort-level value, but the first year is deliberately dilutive and the downside remains negative without verified adoption and incrementality." — abstraction stacked on abstraction.
- "Public filings anchor the baseline. Controlled experiments must earn the forecast." — staged two-beat cadence; meaning must be reverse-engineered.
- "Win with a CFO-sponsored, controller-enabled, savings-backed deployment—not with generic AI messaging." — slogan with stacked compound modifiers.
- "Spend information is fragmented: ERP, AP, cards, procurement, contracts and spreadsheets produce late insight and weak action ownership." — list-heavy compression; "late insight and weak action ownership" is unrecoverable.
- "The market is crowded: feature novelty is insufficient; the launch must prove governed outcomes in the client's own data." — colon-semicolon cadence, abstract jargon.

**Labeled NOT slop (keep sentences like these):**

- "The bug is in the parser, not the tokenizer." — concrete distinction.
- "The 12 June accessibility audit found 14 missing labels." — source and result.
- "The API returns 429 when the client exceeds the rate limit." — technical term, concrete condition.
- "At the 2018 emissions pace, 1.5°C has roughly 10 years left." — number-backed headline.
- "Prioritize carrier quality over new lane expansion or shipper-onboarding automation." — direct recommendation with named alternatives.
- "Q1 2026 Freight gross bookings and revenue were $1.334 billion and $1.337 billion, respectively, but segment operating loss widened to $30 million." — figures.
- "In 2025, revenue fell 1%, while adjusted EBITDA improved 55% as carrier payments and other expenses declined." — figures with cause.
- "Uber Freight reports working with more than 125,000 vetted truckload carriers and one-third of Fortune 500 companies." — attributed figures.
- "In Q1, fraud incidents fell 20% after tighter carrier onboarding, monitoring, and detection." — result plus mechanism.
- "Regional lanes are the next bet only where validated carrier depth already exists." — concrete condition.
- "Optimize unit economics before adding footprint." — terse but recoverable directive.
- "Approve a stage-gated pilot; do not pre-authorize a broad rollout." — decision language with a real distinction.
- "The investment has a modeled three-year net present value of $18 million, using a 12% planning hurdle." — figures and method.
- "The modeled economic payback period is 21 months, including training time." — figure with scope.
- "CFOs need to reduce expenses, improve forecast accuracy, and strengthen financial controls." — plain list of real goals.
- "Identify which executives approve the purchase and which teams will use the product." — actionable and concrete.
- "Restrict nonessential visits; strengthen screening." — plain directive (semicolon separating two real actions).

The dividing line: sentences that survive carry a number, a named source, a real condition, or a decision. Sentences that get flagged carry cadence, abstraction, or compression the reader must decode.

## 7. Formatting and overstyling reference

From the aesthetics rubric (priority order: visual defects > overstyling > skimmability):

**Visual defects (worst — an unusable defect caps the score at 0):** clipped/overlapping/corrupted content, content crossing container boundaries, unreadable text, broken margins/alignment/spacing/page flow, low contrast, broken repeated elements, visible editing residue, manual text-shaped numbering ("1 |") instead of native lists, misaligned tables/callouts.

**Overstyling (remove anything that can be removed without reducing clarity):**

- decorative lines beneath headers, between sections, or at the end of the document
- colored callout blocks, bright accents, colored headings and table fills
- three-part title treatments (eyebrow + main title + subtitle)
- tables where a simple list would be clearer
- zebra striping, multiple fill colors, heavy borders, decorative framing
- charts, diagrams, or images added for decoration rather than understanding
- repeated middle dots, pipes, or decorative separators

Most workplace documents: minimal structure, mostly black text on white, easy to read and edit. Tables, diagrams, and headers default to simple black-and-white with plain borders; use color or shading only when it improves comparison, grouping, or readability. Extra styling is justified only when the user requested it or it is standard for the document type and audience.

**Skimmability:** clear headings, readable paragraphs, useful white space, and native elements (bullets, numbered lists, checkboxes) where they help — judged against the document type. A research report should be skimmable; a legal brief is legitimately dense.

**Overall document judgment:** score holistically, not as an average. A major failure (instruction-following miss, unsupported claim, fabricated or missing required citation, omission that prevents professional use) caps the document regardless of strengths elsewhere. Ask: is it ready for real use? Does it succeed for the apparent audience and purpose? How much revision before it could be confidently shared?
